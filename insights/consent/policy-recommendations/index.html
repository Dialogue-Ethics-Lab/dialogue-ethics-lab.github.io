Consent

We recommend that shared society programs shift toward a model of ongoing consent that recognizes participants', including young people's, ownership over their likeness. Rather than treating consent as a single event, programs should understand it as a continuous, revisitable process that evolves as participants' circumstances, comfort levels, and needs change.

SummaryPolicy RecommendationsActivitiesFrameworks

Participants should be included in programmatic discussions and decisions of how their likeness is used.

A person’s likeness, including visual and audio representations, is an extension of the self, and therefore, belongs to the person. Including participants in decision-making about how their likeness will be used is a right, and develops critical thinking about how personal representations in visual and audio form are created, shared, altered, and used. This work is an important part of creating a culture of consent within a program. These decisions and discussions can happen at the start of the year, or in reflection of the program year.

Educational programming should be offered at the start of the program year (and throughout if possible) to support youth and staff to understand the language of consent and practice the act of consenting.

Giving consent requires active learning through conversations and simulations, where students can practice the act of giving consent, not giving consent, and revoking consent as well, even after giving it initially. Educational programming should support youth and staff to reflect on how those experiences feel and recognize how power dynamics evolve across relationships and contexts.

Each program should provide guidelines about how to define consent and offer opportunities to dialogue about what is consent for both staff and participants.

Establishing clear guidelines about consent helps youth and staff develop a shared understanding of the boundaries that create a program of connection, safety, and community. Guidelines also create opportunities for ongoing conversations about consent, making it easier to recognize, discuss, and respond to harm when it occurs.

Programs have a responsibility to ensure that youth and staff understand the realities of surveillance, and have clear and meaningful choices about how and when their likeness is used on social media and in donor appeals.

Surveillance should be understood as both the experience of being surveilled and the feeling of being surveilled. Although it can affect anyone, surveillance is not experienced equally. An intersectional perspective recognizes that Israelis and Palestinians experience surveillance differently depending on their intersecting identities, with some individuals facing heightened surveillance and greater risks than others.
